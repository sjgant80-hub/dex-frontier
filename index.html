The Self-Growing DEX Frontier

Frontier AI is poured: every record into one giant model in one data centre, rented back to you. This grows it instead. Each department keeps its own records. A seed of a few hundred bytes grows a specialist from one department’s records, on that department’s machine, and it must prove itself on cases it never saw or it is cut. The specialists share answers, never records. A coupling learns from outcomes how much each one’s word is worth, and the mesh answers as one mind.

Everything on this page runs the real, mutation-gated code in your browser, on two real public datasets. The one claim it all rests on — that specialists compose into more than the sum — was sealed before it was measured, and allowed to fail.

00What it is, in plain words

New to the words? They are explained at the end of this section.

Every frontier model today is built the same way: gather all the data in one place, train one enormous model on it, and rent out the answers. Your records go to it, or it never sees them. This page builds the opposite way. A business already keeps its records apart: billing has billing’s, the product team has usage, the CRM has the customer. Each of those silos grows its own specialist node, on its own machine, from a seed: a signed recipe of a few hundred bytes that says what to look for. The node sets every threshold from its own records and must clear a bar on past cases it never trained on, or it is cut and says nothing.

The live nodes then answer together. Each scores the case from what its own silo knows, and a coupling (κ) weighs their scores into one answer. It learned those weights from outcomes on cases none of them trained on. Nothing but scores crosses between nodes. The billing node never sees the CRM, and the CRM never sees billing.

The spec this was built from says the whole idea rests on one claim, to be proven before anything scales: do specialists compose into more than the sum? Organ 2 is that proof, on real data, sealed before it ran and allowed to fail.

The words this page uses

Silo
— the records one department keeps, and only those.
Seed
— a signed recipe of a few hundred bytes: which silo, and how hard to search. It carries no trained weights.
Node
— what a seed grows on one silo’s records: a handful of readable rules, each a column, a cut and a weight.
Gate
— the bar a node must clear on past cases it never trained on before it may answer at all.
Cut
— a node that did not clear its bar. It answers nothing.
Coupling (κ)
— how much each node’s word is worth, learned from outcomes on cases no node trained on.
Composed
— the mesh’s one answer: the nodes’ scores, weighed by the coupling.
Poured, monolith
— the usual way: every record in one place and one model fitted on all of it.
Sealed
— rules written and published before a measurement ran, so they could not be bent to the result.
Prediction, property
— a sealed rule that may fail (and is kept when it does), or one the build promises must hold.
Mutation gate
— the code is deliberately broken, line by line, and the tests must notice every break.
AUC
— how often a real case is ranked above a non-case: 0.5 is a coin toss, 1 is perfect.

03How it works

  1. A silo stays where it is. Each department’s records stay on its own machine. The data on this page is cut the same way: a telecom company’s 7,043 real customers into customer (4 columns), services (9) and account (7); a web shop’s 12,330 real sessions into browsing (6), analytics (3) and context (7).
  2. A seed grows a node. The contributor seals a seed for their silo. On their machine, the seed-library ribosome searches for the rules that predict best on part of the silo’s own records and fits every threshold to them. This is a search, not gradient training, and the result is a handful of readable rules.
  3. The gate decides. The node is scored on the silo’s GATE records, which it never grew on. Under the bar, it is cut and abstains on everything.
  4. The coupling learns. On the same GATE records, a small logistic regression learns how much each live node’s score is worth. That is the κ-coupling, and it is learned from outcomes, not set by hand.
  5. The mesh answers. A new case goes only to the live nodes whose silo it carries (routing by fit). Each returns a score, and the coupling weighs them into one answer, with every rule that fired. Records never move.

05Organ 2 · The composition proof

The critical gate. Nothing in the spec scales until this holds: do specialists, each grown on one silo and coupled at κ, compose into more than the sum?

In each of two real domains, every silo grows its own node and the live ones are coupled. The composed mesh is then measured on test cases none of them saw, against four kinds of rival. The first is its best single node: the best on the test cases themselves, the strictest rival there is. The second is the shams: random routing, a coupling taught shuffled outcomes, and a mesh grown on shuffled outcomes. The third is the poured monolith: boosted trees and logistic regression on every column, with every record pooled in one place. The fourth is the 14B and the frontier model, on 300 cases each.

Sealed, being measured. The rules were committed before a single TEST case was scored. The 14B is answering on this laptop’s processor, one case at a time; the frontier model has answered. This fills in from the record when it is judged.

Operations

Deployment and roll-out

  1. Pick one question whose answer lives across two or three departments’ records (churn, fraud, a referral, a fault).
  2. Each department makes its own key, offline, and seals a seed for its own columns.
  3. Each grows its node on its own machine, from its own records, and holds it to its bar on cases it never grew on.
  4. Learn the coupling on a held-back slice of outcomes that every department can score. Only the scores meet.
  5. Run it beside your current process, and compare its calls with what happened.
  6. Add departments: each one grows its own node, and the coupling re-learns.

Risks, answered straight

Is it accurate enough?

It is being measured now, on two real public datasets.

Is this the frontier language model the spec describes?

Not yet, and this page does not claim it. What is proven here is the mechanism on structured records: specialists grown apart, composed into one answer. Whether the same composition holds for language is the next question, not this one’s answer.

Does coordination cost more than it saves?

Coordination here is one score per case per department, and a coupling of a few numbers learned in milliseconds. The heavy cost of the usual way, gathering everything into one place, is the step this removes.

Has anyone outside tried to break it?

Not yet. The repository, its tests and its mutation gate are open; report a break as a GitHub issue.

09White paper

The self-growing DEX frontier: specialists grown on separate departments’ records, composed into one answer, with the composition proof sealed before it ran.

Abstract

Frontier models are built by pooling every record in one place. We test the opposite construction on two real public datasets, each cut into the silos a business keeps apart. Each silo grows a specialist node from a signed seed of a few hundred bytes, by gated search on its own records. A coupling learned from held-back outcomes (stacked generalisation over the nodes’ scores) combines the live nodes into one answer, and records never cross. Every claim was pre-registered: rules and the exact bytes of every file were committed before any test case was scored, and git proves the order. The measurement is under way; this abstract fills in from its record.

1 · The construction inversion

Poured: all data to one model in one place, trained once, rented. Grown: each department grows its own specialist on its own machine; the specialists compose. The grown form keeps records where they are, lets a department join or leave without retraining the rest, and makes every rule readable. Whether it also keeps the accuracy is the open question, and it is what organ 2 measures.

2 · Method

Two datasets: IBM’s Telco Customer Churn sample (7,043 customers; silos: customer 4 columns, services 9, account 7; FIT / GATE / TEST stratified, 3,699 / 1,232 / 2,112) and the UCI Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention dataset (12,330 sessions; browsing 6, analytics 3, context 7; FIT / GATE from February–October, TEST the 4,725 November–December sessions). A node is the seed-library ribosome’s gated evolutionary search (population 40, elites 8, generations 30) over up to six threshold rules, fitted on its silo’s FIT rows, and cut unless it scores AUC 0.6 on GATE. The coupling is an L2 logistic regression on the live nodes’ standardised GATE scores. Rivals: the best node on TEST, random routing, a coupling taught shuffled outcomes, a mesh grown on shuffled outcomes, boosted trees and logistic regression on every column pooled, and two language models on 300 cases per domain. Bounds: paired bootstrap, 2,000 resamples. Every kernel is mutation-gated; every record is re-derived by CI.

5 · Limitations

  • Two datasets, both tabular; nothing here is a language task.
  • The silos are cut from one table per company, so the departments describe the same customers: the vertically partitioned setting, not every federation.
  • The coupling needs a held-back slice of outcomes every department can score.
  • The language-model comparisons are on 300 cases per domain, and the frontier model saw all 300 at once (an advantage it was given).
  • No outside adversary has tried to break it.

10Questions

What is it?

A way to build one AI from many departments without pooling their records. Each department grows a small specialist from its own records, on its own machine, from a signed seed of a few hundred bytes. A specialist that cannot prove itself on cases it never trained on is cut. The live ones answer together: each scores the case from what it knows, and a coupling learned from outcomes weighs their scores into one answer.

Is this a large language model?

No. What runs here answers questions about structured business records: will this customer leave, will this visitor buy. Each specialist is a handful of readable rules. The spec it was built from aims, in the end, at a distributed frontier language model grown the same way. What is proven here, on real data, is the step the spec says must come first: whether specialists compose into more than the sum.

Do any records leave a department?

No. A node reads only its own silo’s columns, on its own machine, and what crosses between nodes is a score per case. The page enforces the same in your browser: its security policy lets it connect only to its own site, and the data it uses are its own files.

Did composition work?

It is being measured now. The rules were sealed before a single test case was scored. This answer fills in from the record when it is judged.

How does it compare with putting every record in one place?

It is being measured now: the composed mesh against boosted trees and logistic regression with every record pooled in one place.

How does it compare with a 14-billion-parameter model and a frontier model?

Both have answered or are answering now, on 300 test cases per domain: the local 14B one case at a time, the frontier model all 300 at once.

How is this different from federated learning?

Federated learning sends model updates, weights or gradients, from each party to a centre that averages them. Here each department’s records hold different columns about the same customers (the setting known as vertically partitioned data), and nothing is averaged. Each node is grown on its own columns, and what crosses is one score per case. The coupling is stacked generalisation, a known technique: a small model learns how to weigh the specialists’ answers.

What if one department’s records are poor?

Its node is cut. It must clear its bar on cases it never grew on, or it answers nothing and the rest carry on. On this page the shop’s context silo (browser, region, traffic source, visitor type) is that case: it was cut at its gate.

Can a department leave, or join, without retraining everything?

Yes. A node is unplugged by dropping it, and the coupling re-learns its few weights from held-back outcomes in milliseconds; no other node changes. Box 5 does it live. A new department grows its own node from its own seed and joins the same way.

Can the nodes improve themselves?

Two ways are built: breeding, where a child is born only if it beats both parents on cases neither grew on, and growing as records arrive. By the spec’s order, both are measured only after the composition proof is judged.

What happens if a department’s node goes bad, or is taken over?

The mesh is built to re-learn its coupling from outcomes and to prune a node that stops adding (organ 5). It is measured once the composition proof is judged, with the strongest node broken half-way through a real stream.

Does it keep working as more departments join?

Organ 6 cuts the same records finer (7 and 6 silos) and adds the nodes one at a time. It is measured once the composition proof is judged.

Who owns a node, and can they take it back?

The contributor whose key sealed it. Organ 7 measures the rest: a node carried as a card, a ledger of each node’s share, and revoking a node without touching anyone else’s.

What did not work?

Nothing is measured yet. Every failed prediction will be kept on this page with its reason.

How do I check it myself?

In this browser: box 2 grows all six nodes and checks them against CI’s record, and box 4 re-scores every test case. On your own machine, with Node 20 or later and the repository: npm test runs every test; node witness-all.mjs breaks every kernel line by line and checks the tests notice; node tools/measure-2.mjs --check re-derives the composition proof byte for byte. It takes someone who can run a command line.
