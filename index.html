The Self-Growing DEX Frontier

Frontier AI is poured: every record into one giant model in one data centre, rented back to you. This grows it instead. Each department keeps its own records. A seed of a few hundred bytes grows a specialist from one department’s records, on that department’s machine, and it must prove itself on cases it never saw or it is cut. The specialists share answers, never records. A coupling learns from outcomes how much each one’s word is worth, and the mesh answers as one mind.

Everything on this page runs the real, mutation-gated code in your browser, on two real public datasets. The one claim it all rests on — that specialists compose into more than the sum — was sealed before it was measured, and allowed to fail.

00What it is, in plain words

New to the words? They are explained at the end of this section.

Every frontier model today is built the same way: gather all the data in one place, train one enormous model on it, and rent out the answers. Your records go to it, or it never sees them. This page builds the opposite way. A business already keeps its records apart: billing has billing’s, the product team has usage, the CRM has the customer. Each of those silos grows its own specialist node, on its own machine, from a seed: a signed recipe of a few hundred bytes that says what to look for. The node sets every threshold from its own records and must clear a bar on past cases it never trained on, or it is cut and says nothing.

The live nodes then answer together. Each scores the case from what its own silo knows, and a coupling (κ) weighs their scores into one answer. It learned those weights from outcomes on cases none of them trained on. Nothing but scores crosses between nodes. The billing node never sees the CRM, and the CRM never sees billing.

The spec this was built from says the whole idea rests on one claim, to be proven before anything scales: do specialists compose into more than the sum? Organ 2 took that proof first, and every attempt after it (2b onward) took it again, each on real data, sealed before it ran and allowed to fail. The gate says where it stands.

The words this page uses

Silo, department
— the records one department keeps, and only those.
Seed
— a signed recipe of a few hundred bytes: which silo, and how hard to search. It carries no trained weights.
Node
— what a seed grows on one silo’s records: a handful of readable rules, each a column, a cut and a weight (from organ 2i, a few hundred two-level trees instead, where they do better on held-back cases).
Bar, join
— what a node must show on past cases it never trained on before it may answer at all. Up to organ 2g, a fixed score of 0.6; from organ 2h, beating a coin beyond the noise.
Cut
— a node that did not clear its bar. It answers nothing.
The gate
— the spec’s three tests, all of which must hold before anything scales: the mesh beats any single specialist, it comes close to a frontier model, and adding a specialist measurably raises it.
Relay
— departments growing in turn, strongest first, each on its own columns and the scores the stronger ones send it, so a pattern across two departments can be learned while only scores cross (organ 2i).
Mesh
— the live nodes answering together, their scores weighed by the coupling.
Coupling (κ)
— how much each node’s word is worth, learned from outcomes on cases no node trained on.
Composed
— the mesh’s one answer: the nodes’ scores, weighed by the coupling.
Poured, monolith
— the usual way: every record in one place and one model fitted on all of it.
Frontier model
— one of the largest general AI models available (here Claude Opus 5.5), shown every column of a case in words and asked for a probability, cold.
FIT, GATE, TEST
— the three slices of every dataset: nodes grow on FIT, prove themselves and learn the coupling on GATE, and are judged on TEST, which nothing sees until the sealed measurement.
Organ
— one sealed experiment, numbered in the spec’s build order (2b, 2c … are further attempts at organ 2).
Sealed
— rules written and published before a measurement ran, so they could not be bent to the result.
Prediction, property
— a sealed rule that may fail (and is kept when it does), or one the build promises must hold.
Mutation gate
— the code is deliberately broken, line by line, and the tests must notice every break.
AUC
— how often a real case is ranked above a non-case: 0.5 is a coin toss, 1 is perfect. It does not depend on how rare the outcome is.
Bound, beyond the noise
— a lead is real only if it survives resampling the cases two thousand times: the bound is the lead the worst one in forty of those resamples still shows.
Stacked generalisation
— the known technique the coupling is: a small model learns how to weigh other models’ answers (Wolpert, 1992).
Living Seed
— the earlier build whose code grows each node: a signed seed that germinates into a working, checked set of rules on the records it finds.
Breeding
— two nodes of one silo crossed into a child, kept only if it beats both parents on cases neither trained on (organ 3, built; by the spec’s order sealed only after the gate is won).

03How it works

  1. A silo stays where it is. Each department’s records stay on its own machine. The data on this page is cut the same way: a telecom company’s 7,043 real customers into customer (4 columns), services (9) and account (7); a web shop’s 12,330 real sessions into browsing (6), analytics (3) and context (7).
  2. A seed grows a node. The contributor seals a seed for their silo. On their machine, the seed-library ribosome searches for the rules that predict best on part of the silo’s own records and fits every threshold to them. This is a search, not gradient training, and the result is a handful of readable rules. From organ 2i a department may instead field depth-2 boosted trees grown on the same records, when they do better on cases it never grew on: the same learner the pooled rival uses, so the comparison with every record pooled is like for like.
  3. The gate decides. The node is scored on the silo’s GATE records, which it never grew on. Under the bar, it is cut and abstains on everything.
  4. The coupling learns. On the same GATE records, a small logistic regression learns how much each live node’s score is worth. That is the κ-coupling, and it is learned from outcomes, not set by hand.
  5. The mesh answers. A new case goes only to the live nodes whose silo it carries (routing by fit). Each returns a score, and the coupling weighs them into one answer, with every rule that fired. Records never move.

04Organ 1 · One specialist body

The spec’s first step: does one node, grown from a seed on one department’s records, beat a big generalist on that department’s question?

The node is the billing department’s: the telecom company’s 7 account columns. Its seed is 311 bytes and its body is 6 rules. Against it, on 300 test customers it never saw, are two contestants that see all 20 columns. One is qwen2.5:14b on this laptop’s processor, shown 24 past customers with their outcomes. The other is a frontier model (Claude Opus 5.5), asked cold with the same examples.

The verdict. The billing department’s node, seeing 7 of the 20 columns, beat the 14B that saw all 20 beyond the noise (0.801 against 0.741, bound +0.014), and it fell more than 0.02 behind the frontier model (0.844, bound −0.067). A3 failed as sealed, and is kept.

05Organ 2 · The composition proof

The critical gate. Nothing in the spec scales until this holds: do specialists, each grown on one silo and coupled at κ, compose into more than the sum?

In each of two real domains, every silo grows its own node and the live ones are coupled. The composed mesh is then measured on test cases none of them saw, against four kinds of rival. The first is its best single node: the best on the test cases themselves, the strictest rival there is. The second is the shams: random routing, a coupling taught shuffled outcomes, and a mesh grown on shuffled outcomes. The third is the poured monolith: boosted trees and logistic regression on every column, with every record pooled in one place. The fourth is the 14B and the frontier model, on 300 cases each.

The verdict on the gate. Composition held on the telco customers and did not on the shop sessions: there, the coupled mesh did not beat its best single node beyond the noise. The sealed prediction (B2) failed and is kept. By the spec’s own rule, the next organ fixes composition before anything scales.

The gate, taken 10 times, every attempt sealed first: held once, on one fresh domain, not yet repeated

The first test first held in both domains in organ 2e: the mesh chooses its own coupling, linear or trees, out of fold on the cases it may learn from. 06What scales next, and only after the gate

The spec’s order is fixed: one body, then the composition proof, then breeding, self-growing, routing that learns, scale, and sovereign spread. Each later organ is sealed only once composition wins all three of the gate’s tests. Until it does, the next organ fixes composition first.

06bOrgans 2b–2j · Fixing composition, sealed each time

New to the words below (node, mesh, coupling, bar, bound, beyond the noise)? Each is defined in plain words in the words this page uses. In short: a node is one department’s small model, the mesh combines the nodes’ scores into one answer, and a lead is “beyond the noise” only when it survives resampling the cases two thousand times.

Taken only if organ 2 found that composition does not net. A node grown on its own finds what predicts best by itself, so a weaker silo can mostly repeat what the strongest already knows. Grown to complement the mesh instead, does it add?

Operations

Every department’s node shows as live or cut, with the reason. A department can be unplugged without retraining anyone else (box 5), and a new one joins by growing its own node. A node that has not earned its place says nothing rather than misrouting work.

Records never leave their silo; only one score per case crosses. Every seed is signed (Ed25519) and checked before a line of it is read (box 6). Deployment and roll-out

  1. Pick one question whose answer lives across two or three departments’ records (churn, fraud, a referral, a fault).
  2. Each department makes its own key, offline, and seals a seed for its own columns.
  3. Each grows its node on its own machine, from its own records, and holds it to its bar on cases it never grew on.
  4. Learn the coupling on a held-back slice of past cases that every department can score, keyed by a case id they share. Only each department’s scores for those cases and the outcomes meet; never a department’s records.
  5. Run it beside your current process, and compare its calls with what happened.
  6. Add departments: each one grows its own node, and the coupling re-learns.

1 · The construction inversion

Poured: all data to one model in one place, trained once, rented. Grown: each department grows its own specialist on its own machine; the specialists compose. The grown form keeps records where they are, lets a department join or leave without retraining the rest, and makes every rule readable. Whether it also keeps the accuracy is what the composition gate measures, organs 2 to 2e.

2 · Method

Two datasets: IBM’s Telco Customer Churn sample (7,043 customers; silos: customer 4 columns, services 9, account 7; FIT / GATE / TEST stratified, 3,699 / 1,232 / 2,112) and the UCI Online Shoppers Purchasing Intention dataset (12,330 sessions; browsing 6, analytics 3, context 7; FIT / GATE from February–October, TEST the 4,725 November–December sessions). A node is the seed-library ribosome’s gated evolutionary search (population 40, elites 8, generations 30) over up to six threshold rules, fitted on its silo’s FIT rows, and cut unless it scores AUC 0.6 on GATE. The first coupling (organ 2) is an L2 logistic regression on the live nodes’ standardised GATE scores. The one organ 2e found first beats its best node in both domains chooses, by 4-fold out-of-fold AUC on GATE alone, between that and boosted trees over the scores (100 rounds, learning rate 0.1), then learns the chosen one on all of GATE. Rivals: the best node on TEST, random routing, a coupling taught shuffled outcomes, a mesh grown on shuffled outcomes, boosted trees and logistic regression on every column pooled, and two language models on 300 cases per domain. Bounds: paired bootstrap, 2,000 resamples. Every kernel is mutation-gated; every record is re-derived by CI.

5 · Limitations

  • Two datasets, both tabular; nothing here is a language task.
  • The silos are cut from one table per company, so the departments describe the same customers: the vertically partitioned setting, not every federation.
  • The coupling needs a held-back slice of outcomes every department can score.
  • The language-model comparisons are on 300 cases per domain, and the frontier model saw all 300 at once (an advantage it was given).
  • The gate’s first test held on the fifth attempt. Each attempt was sealed before it ran, but all five were scored on the same test cases, so by the fifth those cases were no longer fresh to the line of attempts. A dataset none of them has seen would settle it: organ 2f is that test, on two.
  • No outside adversary has tried to break it.

10Questions

What is it?

A way to build one AI from many departments without pooling their records. Each department grows a small specialist from its own records, on its own machine, from a signed seed of a few hundred bytes. A specialist that cannot prove itself on cases it never trained on is cut. The live ones answer together: each scores the case from what it knows, and a coupling learned from outcomes weighs their scores into one answer.

Is this a large language model?

No. What runs here answers questions about structured business records: will this customer leave, will this visitor buy. Each specialist is a handful of readable rules or, from organ 2i, where they did better on cases it never grew on, a few hundred two-level decision trees grown on that department’s records alone. The spec it was built from aims, in the end, at a distributed frontier language model grown the same way. What is proven here, on real data, is the step the spec says must come first: whether specialists compose into more than the sum.

Do any records leave a department?

No. A node reads only its own silo’s columns, on its own machine, and what crosses between nodes is a score per case. The page enforces the same in your browser: its security policy lets it connect only to its own site, and the data it uses are its own files.

Did composition work?

On one more dataset no attempt had touched, 17,999 answers to the CDC’s health survey held out (organ 2j, a department seated only if the mesh with it beats the mesh without it beyond the noise), 3 of 5 departments offered joined and the mesh scored 0.824. Beating its best department: held (0.824 against 0.779, bound +0.039). Approaching the frontier model on 1,200 cases: held (0.826 against 0.822, bound −0.006). Growing measurably as departments join: held (0.779 → 0.811 → 0.824, bound +0.039). The relay beating the same departments answering alone: failed (0.824 against 0.823, bound −0.001). All three of the gate’s tests held together, on one fresh domain, on the tenth sealed attempt: by its sealed rule the gate holds. The attempts before it asked for two domains each and none held in both, so this is a narrower pass, not yet repeated; and on this survey the signal adds up across departments almost as it does pooled, the kind of domain where one score per department loses least. Before it, organ 2i: On two more datasets no attempt had touched, 17,999 hotel bookings and 11,893 news articles held out (organ 2i, the departments relaying scores to one another), the mesh scored 0.899 and 0.733. Beating its best department: held on the hotel bookings (0.899 against 0.774, bound +0.118), held on the news articles (0.733 against 0.676, bound +0.051). Approaching the frontier model on 1,200 cases: held on the hotel bookings (0.909 against 0.859, bound +0.036), held on the news articles (0.750 against 0.701, bound +0.026). Growing measurably as departments join: held on the hotel bookings (0.774 → 0.836 → 0.876 → 0.899, bound +0.118), failed on the news articles (0.676 → 0.714 → 0.729 → 0.732 → 0.734 → 0.733, bound +0.051). The relay beating the same departments answering alone: held on the hotel bookings (0.899 against 0.889, bound +0.007), held on the news articles (0.733 against 0.727, bound +0.003). So the gate was not won there; every failed test is kept.

How does it compare with putting every record in one place?

On fresh cases, boosted trees with every record pooled in one place against the mesh, whose records never met: credit clients 0.780 against 0.767; bank calls 0.797 against 0.786; census people 0.912 against 0.896. Within 0.02 beyond the noise in all three. On the hospital stays every department was cut at its bar, so no mesh formed (pooled 0.659).

How does it compare with a 14-billion-parameter model and a frontier model?

On 1,200 fresh credit clients the frontier model, Claude Opus 5.5 asked cold, scored 0.746 and the mesh 0.761 on the same cases; on 1,200 hospital stays the frontier model scored 0.631 and every record pooled 0.659, where no mesh formed. In organ 2, on 300 cases per domain, both models saw every column and 24 past cases. The local qwen2.5:14b scored 0.741 (telecom) and 0.725 (shop). The frontier model, Claude Opus 5.5 asked cold, scored 0.844 and 0.858. Organ 2’s linear mesh scored 0.812 and 0.808 on the same cases. Beating the 14B did not hold in both domains; holding within 0.02 of the frontier model did not hold in both.

How is this different from federated learning?

Federated learning sends model updates, weights or gradients, from each party to a centre that averages them. Here each department’s records hold different columns about the same customers (the setting known as vertically partitioned data), and nothing is averaged. Each node is grown on its own columns, and what crosses is one score per case. The coupling is stacked generalisation, a known technique: a small model learns how to weigh the specialists’ answers.

What if one department’s records are poor?

Its node is cut. It must clear its bar on cases it never grew on, or it answers nothing and the rest carry on. On this page the shop’s context silo (browser, region, traffic source, visitor type) is that case: it was cut at its gate.

Can a department leave, or join, without retraining everything?

Yes. A node is unplugged by dropping it, and the coupling re-learns its few weights from held-back outcomes in milliseconds; no other node changes. Box 5 does it live. A new department grows its own node from its own seed and joins the same way.

Can the nodes improve themselves?

Two ways are built: breeding, where a child is born only if it beats both parents on cases neither grew on, and growing as records arrive. By the spec’s order, both are measured only once composition wins the gate, which it has done once, on one fresh domain (organ 2j): they wait until that pass is repeated on a second, then breeding is sealed first.

What happens if a department’s node goes bad, or is taken over?

The mesh is built to re-learn its coupling from outcomes and to prune a node that stops adding (organ 5). It is measured, with the strongest node broken half-way through a real stream, only once composition wins the gate.

Does it keep working as more departments join?

Organ 6 cuts the same records finer (7 and 6 silos) and adds the nodes one at a time. It is measured only once composition wins the gate.

Who owns a node, and can they take it back?

The contributor whose key sealed it. Organ 7 measures the rest: a node carried as a card, a ledger of each node’s share, and revoking a node without touching anyone else’s.

How do I check it myself?

In this browser: box 2 grows all six nodes and checks them against CI’s record, and box 4 re-scores every test case. On your own machine, with Node 20 or later and the repository: npm test runs every test; node witness-all.mjs breaks every kernel line by line and checks the tests notice; node tools/measure-2.mjs --check re-derives the composition proof byte for byte. It takes someone who can run a command line.

Can it see how one department’s columns interact with another’s?

Partly, and this is the price of no record crossing. Each department sends one score per case, so a pattern that needs a column from billing and a column from the CRM together, seen raw, is invisible to both. What the mesh can do is weigh the departments’ scores against each other, and with the tree coupling it can learn that two scores together mean more than either alone. In the checks made on held-back cases alone (FIT and GATE), one boosted model per department, each sending one score, came within a hundredth of every record pooled on 4 of the 8 datasets and within two hundredths on 6; it trailed on the coupon offers by 0.030 (0.753 against 0.784) and on the forest cells by 0.028 (0.844 against 0.872).

Does it work in real time, and at scale?

A node’s answer is a sum over a few dozen rules or, from organ 2i, a few hundred two-level trees, so scoring a case takes at most a few hundred comparisons, and the coupling a few more: fast enough to answer as each case arrives. Box 4 on this page re-scores every test case of both domains in your browser. Growing a node takes seconds to minutes per department on an ordinary machine. A new department grows one more node from its own seed, and the coupling re-learns; no other node changes.

What does a pilot take?

One question whose answer lives across two or three departments, and a few people’s time: someone in each department runs the seal and the grow on their own machine, and someone joins the departments’ scores on a held-back slice of past cases, keyed by a case id they share, to learn the coupling. Then it runs beside your current process while you compare its calls with what happened. The code carries no licence fee; support is agreed per deployment with AI Native Solutions.

How does it connect to the systems we already have?

It reads a table. Each department exports its own columns from whatever system holds them, one row per case, with a case id the departments share. A loader turns each column into a number by a fixed list written down before anything is measured; src/fresh3.mjs holds two, for the coupon survey and the forest cells. Nothing is installed into your systems and nothing writes back to them. What comes out is a score per case id, which you send wherever the decision is made.

What if our records are messy, incomplete, or change over time?

Messy: a blank answer gets a code of its own (the coupon survey’s unanswered habits are coded as not answered), and a value outside a column’s list stops the load, naming the row and the reason, rather than being guessed. Incomplete: a department whose records say little is held to its bar like any other; from organ 2h it joins only if it beats a coin beyond the noise, so a weak department can still add without being trusted blindly. Change over time: the shop sessions were split by time, with nodes grown on February to October and judged on November and December, months none of them saw. Beyond that, nothing on this page proves how it handles drift. When records change, a department re-grows its node from the same seed on newer records, and the node must pass its bar again before it answers.

What if the outcome we care about is rare?

The score used throughout, AUC, does not depend on how rare the outcome is. In the fresh test sets the outcome ran from 9.0% (hospital stays followed by a readmission within 30 days) to 56.8% (coupons accepted). A rare outcome leaves fewer cases to learn from and widens the noise bound, and every sealed test is judged on that bound, so rarity shows up as a wider margin, never as a flattering average.
